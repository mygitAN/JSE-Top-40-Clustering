Interpretable Unsupervised Learning for JSE Top 40 Equities:
A Repeated Cross-Sectional Study of Financial Peer Groups
A. Nxitywa
Journal-neutral working manuscript (revision of original SACJ draft)
15 November 2026
Evidence snapshot: 39f929e66ac8; archived run20261115T062631-0c09819ce3c0
Abstract
This paper investigates whether interpretable unsupervised learning can describe financial
peer groups and changes in their composition across repeated FTSE/JSE Top 40 cross-
sections. We examine three decision dates—30 June 2025, 31 December 2025 and 30 June
2026—with 41 listed instruments (40 economic company groups) at each date. The union
comprises 43 securities, of which 39 appear at all three dates; hence the study has 123
instrument-date records but not 123 independent firms. The pilot-informed representation
comprises eight financial characteristics designed to reduce share-subdivision sensitivity. We
fit a two-dimensional UMAP embedding followed by average-linkage, Manhattan-distance
agglomerative clustering into eight groups at each date, using fixed parameters and five random
seeds. The original paper’s t-SNE/hierarchical specification is retained as a comparator. The
UMAP clusters have silhouette scores of 0.685, 0.638 and 0.721 in their learned embeddings,
but only 0.151, 0.124 and 0.267 in the scaled financial features. Jaccard-aligned memberships
identify 15 and 11 changes among 40 continuing securities in adjacent periods; across the
39 continuously observed securities, 19 remain in their aligned group throughout and 20
shift at least once. These changes are descriptive, not independently validated structural or
economic anomalies. Historical source reconstruction, small cluster sizes, seed sensitivity and
incomplete external validation constrain inference. The study supplies a transparent workflow
for repeated equity screening while showing why visually separated maps are insufficient
evidence of meaningful financial groups.
Keywords: equity screening; JSE Top 40; clustering; UMAP; temporal membership; Jaccard
similarity; financial feature engineering; interpretability.Status of this manuscript: journal-
neutral working draft; derived from an exploratory, retrospective dataset. The text does not
represent a submission-ready or point-in-time-certified empirical finding.
1 Introduction
Financial markets generate multivariate data that are difficult to interpret using sector labels
alone. Equity analysts commonly organise companies by sector classification, but firms within
the same sector can differ in risk, liquidity, valuation, return behaviour and scale. Conversely,
companies in different sectors may behave similarly because they share economic drivers, investor
sentiment, balance-sheet structures or exposure to market cycles. These cross-sector relationships
can be useful for portfolio screening, risk assessment and deeper analyst investigation.
Much of the machine learning literature in stock-market analysis focuses on supervised prediction,
especially forecasting prices, returns or buy-sell signals. Such tasks are difficult because stock
prices are noisy, non-stationary and influenced by macroeconomic news, firm announcements,

liquidity changes and behavioural factors. A complementary question is not whether a model
can predict the next price, but whether it can reveal hidden structure in a market at a point
in time. This question is well suited to unsupervised learning because no outcome labels are
required during model fitting.
This article studies the Johannesburg Stock Exchange (JSE) Top 40 as a South African emerging-
market case. The JSE, established in 1887, is a major African equity exchange. The JSE
Top 40 contains large, liquid companies across multiple sectors but remains small enough
for detailed post-clustering interpretation. The objective is not to produce an automated
trading rule. It is to evaluate whether a transparent unsupervised learning pipeline can identify
coherent financial peer groupings and describe how those relationships differ across decision
dates. This is important because several JSE Top 40 firms have global revenue, commodity,
banking, telecommunications or retail exposure, making sector groupings useful but incomplete
descriptions of multivariate financial similarity. In this repeated study, sector labels serve as an
external descriptive comparator, not as verified historical ground truth.
The approach proposed by the paper is fourfold. First, we examine financial characteristics
and index membership at three decision dates: 30 June 2025, 31 December 2025 and 30 June
2026. Second, we apply one pilot-informed UMAP–hierarchical pipeline, retaining the previous
paper’s t-SNE–hierarchical configuration as a comparator and reporting clustering quality both
in the original scaled financial-feature space and in the reduced map. Third, we match clusters
across adjacent dates using continuing-member Jaccard overlap, report the results for all 41
instruments per date, and trace complete histories for the 39 instruments present throughout.
Fourth, we examine directly observable cluster profiles and feature differences, with surrogate
SHAP and a separate multivariate-distance screen treated as qualified descriptive evidence rather
than causal explanations or confirmed anomalies. The research question is:How consistently can
interpretable unsupervised learning identify financial peer groups within the JSE Top 40, and
what descriptive changes in peer-group composition and measured financial characteristics occur
across repeated cross-sections?
The article is positioned as exploratory financial data mining rather than predictive modelling. It
does not claim to forecast future returns, determine fair value or generate a trading strategy. Its
purpose is to show how a reproducible unsupervised workflow can support analyst attention by
highlighting financial similarity patterns and changes in peer-group relationships, while acknowl-
edging the sensitivity of these patterns to feature design, model fitting and data provenance.
Section 2 reviews related work, Section 3 describes the dated universe and inputs, Section 4 gives
the method, Section 5 presents the empirical results, Section 6 discusses their implications, and
Section 7 concludes.
2 Related Work
2.1 Unsupervised learning in financial markets
Unsupervised learning is used when the goal is to discover structure rather than predict a known
target. Clustering groups assets that are similar under a chosen feature representation and
distance measure. In finance, this allows assets to be compared on returns, volatility, liquidity,
valuation and fundamentals without assuming that industry labels fully describe behaviour
Greene et al. (2008). Prior studies show that clustering can identify market regimes, volatility
conditions and cross-asset relationships. Barradas et al. used PCA and K-Means to detect
stock-market conditions associated with volatility Barradas et al. (2023). Chen et al. found that
return-based commodity clusters aligned with market categories, while volatility-based clusters
identified distress periods Chen et al. (2021). Leon et al. used agglomerative clustering to study
how global equity-market structure changed after the global financial crisis León et al. (2017).

Clustering also appears in portfolio-management and trading contexts. Lim and Ong used shape-
based clustering for Singapore stocks and argued that price-pattern groupings can supplement
industry-based diversification Lim and Ong (2021). Cuomo et al. proposed an unsupervised
framework for market-neutral portfolios Cuomo et al. (2022). Unlu and Xanthopoulos combined
ensemble clustering with modern portfolio theory to reduce clustering variance Ünlü and Xan-
thopoulos (2021). Han et al. used agglomerative clustering for pairs trading Han et al. (2023),
and Nair et al. clustered stock price time series to produce trading recommendations Nair et al.
(2017). These studies show that clustering can reveal economically meaningful groupings, but
they also underline the need for interpretation and validation: useful clusters are not necessarily
self-explanatory.
2.2 Anomaly detection, validation and interpretability
Anomaly detection in finance matters because outlying observations may reflect fraud, instability,
unusual liquidity, structural change, hidden risk or unusual opportunity Ahmed et al. (2016).
Ahmed et al. show that local and clustering-based outlier methods can be useful in financial-
market datasets Ahmed et al. (2017). However, an anomaly is not automatically a negative
signal; in equity analysis it may reflect sector exposure, capital structure, global revenue exposure,
growth expectations or recent operational stress. Interpretable anomaly-detection methods have
therefore been proposed to help explain why a firm is flagged Cheong et al. (2021).
Cluster validation is difficult because unsupervised learning does not learn from labelled outcomes.
Internal metrics such as the Silhouette Score and Davies-Bouldin Index are widely used because
they measure compactness and separation. A higher Silhouette Score implies that observations
are closer to their assigned cluster than to alternative clusters Rousseeuw (1987); a lower Davies-
Bouldin Index implies that the clusters are well separated and compact Davies and Bouldin (1979).
Comparative studies caution that no single validation index is sufficient across all settings, so
multiple metrics should be used, as different indices capture distinct aspects of quality Arbelaitz
et al. (2013); Vendramin et al. (2010). Desirable results show both within-cluster homogeneity
and clear separation between clusters, reflected jointly in a high Silhouette Score and a low
Davies-Bouldin Index. Where a credible external classification exists, it can also serve as a
post-hoc ground-truth benchmark. Here, official JSE sector classification plays that role.
Interpretability is particularly important in financial applications because decision-makers need
to understand why assets are grouped together. SHAP (SHapley Additive exPlanations) values,
derived from cooperative game theory, provide consistent feature-attribution scores and are
widely used to explain complex machine-learning models. Merrick and Taly (2020); Rozemberczki
et al. (2022). In this study, SHAP is applied after clustering by training a surrogate classifier to
predict cluster membership from the original variables. The goal is not only to cluster equities
but to explain whether groupings are driven by size, liquidity, valuation, volatility, recent return
or combinations of these variables.
The South African equity market remains underrepresented in this literature. Existing studies
often focus on larger international markets, use a narrow PCA/K-Means pipeline or stop at
visual cluster explanation Nourahmadi et al. (2022). The gap addressed here is the need for
a compact, interpretable and measurable unsupervised learning workflow for a South African
equity universe.

4 Research methods
4.1 Frozen cross-date preprocessing and primary model
Each variable is scaled using MinMax parameters estimated from the 41 instruments at 30 June
2025. The same scaling is applied, without clipping, at the later dates; later values can thus
lie outside [0, 1]. This preserves a common numeric reference but does not guarantee that one
feature contributes the same share of variance at every date. The source archive reports feature
dominance and comparison with the original ten-feature, log-size ten-feature and market-only
5

five-feature variants. Scores computed in different feature spaces are not directly compared as
though their units were common.
The current study model is UMAP (McInnes et al., 2018) in two dimensions withn_neighbors=3,
min_dist=0.01, Euclidean input distance, followed by AgglomerativeClustering on those
coordinates with eight groups, average linkage and Manhattan distance. The implementation
uses umap-learn 0.5.12 and scikit-learn 1.6.1 . Seeds 42–46 are fitted independently on
each date, with seed 42 reported as the representative partition. The model parameters do not
change between dates, but the representation and partition arerefitted to each cross-section, so
this is not out-of-sample prediction.

eight natural financial groups.
4.2 Partition comparison, seed agreement and cross-date alignment
Let si denote the silhouette of securityi computed under an explicitly named Euclidean distance
geometry. Both the mean silhouette and the Davies–Bouldin index are calculated (i) on the
two-dimensional representation and (ii) on the same partition evaluated in the scaled financial-
feature space. Because UMAP and t-SNE can compress nearby observations and alter global
geometry, large embedding-space scores are presented as a property of the representation rather
than direct validation of economic peer structure.
Five-seed robustness is summarised using all pairwise Adjusted Rand Indices (ARI) between
partitions within a date. For temporal comparison, only instruments present in both adjacent
snapshots are eligible. Given an old clusterAi and a new clusterBj, the overlap is
J(Ai, Bj) = |Ai ∩ Bj||Ai ∪ Bj| , (1)
where both sets are restricted to securities observed at both dates. A Hungarian assignment
matches clusters using Jaccard overlap; zero-overlap groups receive new descriptive identifiers.
This avoids assuming that arbitrary numeric cluster IDs have constant economic meaning. Entry
and exit events are never interpreted as regular peer-group switches.
A security is recorded asshifted when it moves to a different Jaccard-aligned cluster. This
indicator alone cannot distinguish financial change from re-fitting noise. Same-date seed stability
is the only available run-level sensitivity measure; a dedicated perturbation-based structural
noise floor and external economic co-movement test remain future validation tasks.

4.3 Interpretation and distance screening
Cluster definitions are summarised by the difference between their median financial features and
the same-date cross-sectional medians, expressed in the frozen June-2025 scaling. Such profiles
are descriptive properties of the groups. An XGBoost classifier approximates the cluster labels
for optional SHAP-based interpretation (Merrick and Taly, 2020); its classification fidelity is
disclosed out of sample wherever estimable. Low fidelity does not allow strongly worded SHAP
explanations.
A separate exploratory financial-feature distance screen uses squared Mahalanobis distance and a
reference based on the internal individual-distance beta distribution under idealised multivariate
normal assumptions. Heterogeneous equity cross-sections and imputed or clipped financial data
do not justify interpreting these flags as calibrated hypothesis-test discoveries. Sector mismatch,
cluster isolation, peer-group movement and multivariate distance are kept distinct; the present
paper does not claim confirmed economic outliers from their conjunction.

5.3 What changed in the financial profiles?
The clustering output includes company names and feature profiles, which allow a limited
descriptive interpretation. Consider the precious-metals grouping. In June 2025, AngloGold
Ashanti, Gold Fields and Harmony appeared with a wider set of securities; in December 2025
they were grouped with Impala Platinum, Northam, Sibanye-Stillwater and Valterra. That
December group has a relatively high one-year return, beta and volatility in the current scaled
inputs. Its composition is largely retained in June 2026, and the newly admitted Pan African
Resources is observed within that group. These statements describe recorded attributes and
memberships, not a causal effect of commodity prices or evidence of a future return signal.
A second informative example is the group containing ANH, BHG and CFR, which is present
together at all three dates. BTI joins this peer group in December 2025, while PRX shifts
to a different group by June 2026. The June 2026 cluster median profile has high log market
capitalisation and low log share turnover relative to the cross-sectional medians. It illustrates
how a repeated financial-screening procedure can nominate cases for further investigation without
declaring any company’s inherited industry label incorrect.

Profile terms are expressed in units of the frozen June-2025 MinMax reference, with both positive
and negative values. These direct calculations are easier to verify than individual-class SHAP
explanations when the surrogate is unstable.

5.4 Preliminary financial distance screen and absence of external validation
The saved financial-feature distance screen identifies candidate unusual profiles at each date. Its
current descriptive flags include five security-date observations in June 2025, five in December
2025 and four in June 2026. These should not be read as independent discovery events or
calibrated economic anomalies: the threshold depends on a distributional reference that does
not automatically hold for these securities, and several inputs have reconstruction or imputation
caveats. Likewise, the three-date “emerging”, “persistent” and “resolving” vocabulary is available
as a reporting language but does not itself demonstrate a robust economic phenomenon.
No forward-return co-movement benchmark, bootstrap-calibrated transition noise floor, or fully
point-in-time-certified financial source has been established for the current run. These absences
explicitly constrain the results that can be claimed.
6 Discussion
The central methodological finding is a disagreement between attractive two-dimensional maps
and the considerably weaker geometry of the underlying financial-feature space. This is not
a failure of plotting: local representation algorithms deliberately trade aspects of the original
geometry for visibility and local structure. It is, however, a reason to avoid interpreting compact
coloured islands as validated financial peer groups. The chosen UMAP representation uses only
three neighbours and a small minimum-distance setting, both of which emphasise fine-grained
local structure. A reviewer should be shown the original paper’s t-SNE comparator and the
financial-feature silhouette in the same figure, as in Fig. 2.

The study’s longitudinal analysis does provide more than the original single-window evaluation.
By using effective-dated membership and a reproducible Jaccard assignment, it separates security
entry/exit from existing-security peer-group movement and avoids mechanically treating unrelated
integer cluster labels as changes. But the 19 stable and 20 shifted histories are conditional on the
chosen feature representation, fixedk = 8, UMAP seeds and matching procedure. The observed
seed ARI provides a warning about fitting variability; it is not a perturbation-based bound on
the false discovery of a transition. The adjacent periods also share six months of trailing market
information and often the same issuer accounts.
Data definitions are consequential. Raw share count and unadjusted EPS can change mechanically
through subdivisions; the current feature design removes the direct raw share-count input and uses
corrected scale-free ratios, but residual corporate-action, cross-listing and as-of-data risks remain.
Notably, JSE-only volume for primarily overseas listings is an incomplete measure of total trading
liquidity, and the Investec December share allocation contains an interpolation. These concerns
motivate reporting alternate feature sets and not presenting one observed movement—especially
where share-count adjustments are large—as unambiguous economic change.
Several choices are pilot-informed rather than prospectively selected. In particular, the use of
UMAP with three neighbours and minimum distance 0.01 reflects earlier exploration, and the
eight-cluster count is retained for comparison with the predecessor study rather than proven by
an independent count-selection procedure. We avoid calling the legacy sector labels “ground
truth” because their historical classification basis remains partly reconstructed. A stronger
contribution would additionally show a null reference for group structure, perturbation stability
for firm transitions, and external economic relevance, such as subsequent within-cluster return
co-movement relative to sector-defined and matched random groupings. Those tests have not yet
been completed and are not claimed here.
The contribution that survives these qualifications is a transparent, repeatablescreening and
reporting process: a fixed date-effective universe; declared features and evidence flags; explicit
double-geometry quality reporting; reproducible peer-group alignment; and company-level profile
comparisons. Its immediate utility is to prioritise questions for analyst scrutiny, not to automate
investment decisions. This is the strongest defensible positioning for the present empirical
evidence.
7 Conclusion
We applied an interpretable repeated cross-sectional clustering workflow to three FTSE/JSE Top
40 decision dates with 41 listed instruments at each date. The full universe comprises 43 ticker
lines, 39 of which are observed throughout. The Jaccard-aligned UMAP/hierarchical analysis
describes 19 continuously observed instruments that retain their aligned financial peer group and
20 that change group at least once. These descriptive movements warrant attention but do not
establish economically meaningful structural anomalies. Despite high UMAP embedding-space
silhouettes, financial-feature silhouettes remain modest, and the temporal outputs are sensitive
to representation and data provenance. The current evidence supports a transparent screening
methodology, not return predictability or validated persistent anomalies. The next scientific
milestone is independent point-in-time data verification, a perturbation-based noise floor for
peer-group changes and an external financial co-movement benchmark. The article remains a
working, journal-neutral manuscript pending those tests.
