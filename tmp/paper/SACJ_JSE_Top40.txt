ABSTRACT
This study investigates interpretable unsupervised machine learning for identifying patterns and sector-divergent
anomalies among Johannesburg Stock Exchange Top 40 equities. Financial and market variables, including returns,
volatility, trading volume, market capitalisation, earnings per share (EPS) and price-to-earnings (P/E) ratios, were
transformed into a single-year snapshot dataset spanning 6 February 2023 to 5 February 2024. Principal component
analysis, uniform manifold approximation and projection, and t-distributed stochastic neighbour embedding
were combined with K-Means, DBSCAN and hierarchical clustering. Silhouette Score and Davies-Bouldin Index
were used to evaluate cluster quality, while official JSE sector labels were held back as an external ground-truth
benchmark. The strongest result was obtained from t-SNE with hierarchical clustering, achieving a Silhouette
Score of 0.5023 and a Davies-Bouldin Index of 0.5296. A sector-divergent anomaly is defined as a company that
should, by sector ground truth, group with one peer set but instead clusters with another because of shared
financial attributes. SHAP-based surrogate interpretation and Hotelling’s T-squared analysis provide feature-level
explanations and evidence of multivariate outliers.

intelligence; JSE Top 40; financial data mining

1 INTRODUCTION
Financial markets generate multivariate data that are difficult to interpret using sector labels
alone. Equity analysts commonly organise companies by sector classification, but firms within
the same sector can differ in risk, liquidity, valuation, return behaviour and scale. Conversely,
companies in different sectors may behave similarly because they share economic drivers, investor
sentiment, balance-sheet structures or exposure to market cycles. These cross-sector relationships
can be useful for portfolio screening, risk assessment and deeper analyst investigation.

Much of the machine learning literature in stock-market analysis focuses on supervised pre-
diction, especially forecasting prices, returns or buy-sell signals. Such tasks are difficult because
stock prices are noisy, non-stationary and influenced by macroeconomic news, firm announcements,
liquidity changes and behavioural factors. A complementary question is not whether a model
can predict the next price, but whether it can reveal hidden structure in a market at a point in
time. This question is well suited to unsupervised learning because no outcome labels are required
during model fitting.
This article studies the Johannesburg Stock Exchange (JSE) Top 40 as a South African
emerging-market case. The JSE, established in 1887, is the largest stock exchange in Africa by
market capitalisation and ranks among the twenty largest exchanges in the world [ 1]. It has also
been recognised in the World Economic Forum’s Global Competitiveness Report as one of the
best regulated financial markets globally [ 2]. The JSE Top 40 contains large, liquid companies
across multiple sectors but remains small enough for detailed post-clustering interpretation. The
objective is not to produce an automated trading rule. It is to evaluate whether a transparent
unsupervised learning pipeline can identify coherent equity groupings and flag companies whose
financial attributes do not align neatly with expected sector groupings. This is important because
several JSE Top 40 firms have global revenue, commodity, banking, telecommunications or retail
exposure, making official sectors useful for understanding financial behaviour. However, these
sector groupings suffer from incomplete descriptions of multivariate financial similarity.
The approach proposed by the paper is fourfold. First, the study compares combinations of
dimensionality reduction and clustering algorithms on JSE Top 40 financial data. Second, as its
first research contribution, the paper uses internal clustering metrics together with official JSE
sector labels as an external ground-truth benchmark rather than as supervised training labels.
This allows the model to recover expected sector structure and identify sector-divergent anomalies,
where a company is expected to group with its official sector but instead clusters with another
peer group because of shared financial attributes or stock performance. Third, as its second
research contribution, cluster interpretation is combined with SHAP-based feature importance
and Hotelling’s T 2 outlier detection to make the findings more actionable for analysts. Fourth,
the paper explicitly recognises the methodological caveat of clustering on a t-SNE embedding
and uses stability testing and original-variable explanations to reduce the risk of purely visual
conclusions. The research question is:Can interpretable unsupervised machine learning identify
meaningful patterns and sector-divergent anomalies among JSE Top 40 equities?
The article is positioned as exploratory data mining rather than predictive modelling. It
does not claim to forecast future returns, determine fair value or generate a trading strategy. Its
purpose is to show how interpretable unsupervised learning can support analyst attention by
highlighting similarity patterns, sector divergence and multivariate outliers. The remainder of
the paper is structured as follows: Section 2 reviews related work, Section 3 describes the dataset
and methodology, Section 4 presents the results, Section 5 discusses their implications, Section 6
outlines limitations and future work and Section 7 concludes.

2 RELATED WORK
2.1 Unsupervised learning in financial markets
Unsupervised learning is used when the goal is to discover structure rather than predict a known
target. Clustering groups assets that are similar under a chosen feature representation and distance
measure. In finance, this allows assets to be compared on returns, volatility, liquidity, valuation
and fundamentals without assuming that industry labels fully describe behaviour [ 3]. Prior studies
show that clustering can identify market regimes, volatility conditions and cross-asset relationships.
Barradas et al. used PCA and K-Means to detect stock-market conditions associated with volatility
[4]. Chen et al. found that return-based commodity clusters aligned with market categories, while
volatility-based clusters identified distress periods [ 5]. Leon et al. used agglomerative clustering to
study how global equity-market structure changed after the global financial crisis [6].
Clustering also appears in portfolio-management and trading contexts. Lim and Ong used
shape-based clustering for Singapore stocks and argued that price-pattern groupings can supplement
industry-based diversification [7]. Cuomo et al. proposed an unsupervised framework for market-
neutral portfolios [8]. Unlu and Xanthopoulos combined ensemble clustering with modern portfolio
theory to reduce clustering variance [ 9]. Han et al. used agglomerative clustering for pairs
trading [10], and Nair et al. clustered stock price time series to produce trading recommendations
[11]. These studies show that clustering can reveal economically meaningful groupings, but they
also underline the need for interpretation and validation: useful clusters are not necessarily
self-explanatory.
2.2 Anomaly detection, validation and interpretability
Anomaly detection in finance matters because outlying observations may reflect fraud, instability,
unusual liquidity, structural change, hidden risk or unusual opportunity [ 12]. Ahmed et al. show
that local and clustering-based outlier methods can be useful in financial-market datasets [ 13].
However, an anomaly is not automatically a negative signal; in equity analysis it may reflect sector
exposure, capital structure, global revenue exposure, growth expectations or recent operational
stress. Interpretable anomaly-detection methods have therefore been proposed to help explain
why a firm is flagged [14].
Cluster validation is difficult because unsupervised learning does not learn from labelled
outcomes. Internal metrics such as the Silhouette Score and Davies-Bouldin Index are widely
used because they measure compactness and separation. A higher Silhouette Score implies that
observations are closer to their assigned cluster than to alternative clusters [ 15]; a lower Davies-
Bouldin Index implies that the clusters are well separated and compact [ 16]. Comparative studies
caution that no single validation index is sufficient across all settings, so multiple metrics should
be used, as different indices capture distinct aspects of quality [ 17, 18]. Desirable results show
both within-cluster homogeneity and clear separation between clusters, reflected jointly in a high
Silhouette Score and a low Davies-Bouldin Index. Where a credible external classification exists,
it can also serve as a post-hoc ground-truth benchmark. Here, official JSE sector classification

Interpretability is particularly important in financial applications because decision-makers
need to understand why assets are grouped together. SHAP (SHapley Additive exPlanations)
values, derived from cooperative game theory, provide consistent feature-attribution scores and
are widely used to explain complex machine-learning models. [ 19, 20]. In this study, SHAP is
applied after clustering by training a surrogate classifier to predict cluster membership from the
original variables. The goal is not only to cluster equities but to explain whether groupings are
driven by size, liquidity, valuation, volatility, recent return or combinations of these variables.
The South African equity market remains underrepresented in this literature. Existing studies
often focus on larger international markets, use a narrow PCA/K-Means pipeline or stop at visual
cluster explanation [ 21]. The gap addressed here is the need for a compact, interpretable and
measurable unsupervised learning workflow for a South African equity universe.
3 DATA AND METHODOLOGY
3.1 Dataset and feature representation
The historical stock-price dataset spans 6 February 2023 to 5 February 2024, covering approximately
one year of daily trading activity for JSE Top 40 constituents. This provides about one trading
year of observations for calculating one-year price returns, daily returns, annualised volatility
and related risk metrics. The final dataset contains 40 equities and 14 variables, with no missing
values. Three variables (company name, ticker and official JSE sector) were used as identifiers or
external ground-truth information and were excluded from model fitting. The resulting clustering
input data contained 11 financial and market features: analyst summary rating, one-year return,
average trading volume, market capitalisation, beta, price-to-earnings ratio, shares outstanding,
revenue, earnings per share, annualised volatility and dividend yield. The analyst summary rating
is compiled by financial data aggregators from the collective consensus of sell-side equity research
analysts at investment banks and brokerage firms, who issue standardised recommendations on
the expected direction of a stock (Buy, Hold or Sell, or equivalent terms such as Outperform,
Market Perform or Underperform).
The data was compiled from public financial sources, primarily Investing.com, with Yahoo
Finance and listcorp.com used as supplementary references. The equity universe followed the JSE
Top 40 company list provided by the data source at the time of collection. Subsequent verification
indicated that one included company, RMB Holdings Limited (RMH, labelled RMB in the dataset
and figures), a listed investment holding company with property interests, was not an official
FTSE/JSE Top 40 index constituent during the study window; its treatment is discussed in the
results and limitations. Time-derived metrics such as one-year return, average trading volume and
annualised volatility were computed over the window, while fundamental metrics reflect the most
recent reported values available within it.

interpretation, but deliberately excluded from the clustering input to prevent the model from

3.2 Preprocessing, modelling and validation
Company names, tickers and sector labels were removed before modelling. The analyst summary
rating was label-encoded on an ordinal scale, with Buy assigned 1, Hold assigned 0 and Sell assigned
-1. MinMax scaling was applied because variables such as market capitalisation, volume and
revenue have very different magnitudes. A correlation matrix was examined for multicollinearity;
no pairwise correlations exceeded common removal thresholds of 0.8 or 0.9, so all selected features
were retained.
The pipeline combined three dimensionality-reduction methods, namely PCA, t-SNE and
UMAP, with three clustering approaches: K-Means, DBSCAN and hierarchical clustering. PCA
was included as a linear baseline [ 22]. t-SNE and UMAP were included because they can represent
non-linear structures that may appear in financial data [ 23, 24]. Hierarchical clustering was
included because it provides a dendrogram and reveals nested relationships; K-Means provided
a partitional baseline and DBSCAN was tested because it can identify dense regions and noise
points.
PCA retained sufficient components to explain 95% of the variance. t-SNE produced a two-
dimensional embedding and used an automatically managed learning rate. Hierarchical clustering
used average linkage with Manhattan distance, selected after Ward linkage with Euclidean distance

Because the dataset contains eight official JSE sectors, eight clusters were used as the target
configuration for K-Means and hierarchical clustering. This does not mean the model was trained
on sectors; rather, the cluster count was selected to make the unsupervised structure comparable
with the external ground-truth sector benchmark. Under the tuned parameters, DBSCAN did not
consistently produce the required eight-cluster structure.
Clustering was deliberately performed on the two-dimensional t-SNE embedding because it
preserved local neighbourhood structure and produced the clearest separation for interpretation.
It carries a known caveat: distances in t-SNE space are not globally meaningful in the same way as
distances in the scaled feature space. Three safeguards were therefore applied. First, sector labels
were withheld from model fitting. Second, random-seed stability was assessed. Third, feature-level
interpretation was computed from the original variables using a surrogate classifier rather than
from plotted coordinates alone.

Cluster quality was evaluated using the Silhouette Score and Davies-Bouldin Index. Since the
sample contains 40 observations and 11 modelling features, conventional train-test splitting is not
ideal for clustering: a split could create test clusters that do not correspond to training clusters.
Stability analysis was therefore used as a form of internal validation. Five clustering runs were
performed with different random seeds and the consistency of assignments was assessed with the
Adjusted Rand Index (ARI) and Normalised Mutual Information (NMI). Both scores were 1.0
under the tested settings. This indicates that the selected configuration was stable across the
seeds tested, although it does not rule out sensitivity to other hyperparameter choices.
Official JSE sector labels were then used as an external ground-truth benchmark. A cluster
matching sector membership confirms known economic structure. The main anomaly concept
is sector divergence: a company belongs to one JSE sector according to ground truth but is
placed in a cluster dominated by, or mixed with, companies from another sector because its
measured attributes are closer to that group. For example, a consumer goods firm appearing in a
predominantly financial cluster, or a financial firm appearing with telecommunications companies,
is treated as a sector-divergent signal rather than an automatic modelling error.
SHAP explanations were generated using a surrogate gradient-boosted tree classifier trained to
predict cluster membership from the original financial features. The surrogate was used solely
for interpretation rather than prediction, providing a transparent explanation of which original
financial variables distinguished one cluster from another. Explanations were obtained for seven

4.2 Cluster validation against JSE sector ground truth
The selected clustering solution was compared with official JSE sector classifications after model
fitting. Under this benchmark, a sector-coherent result would place many financial firms with
other financial firms, telecommunications firms with telecommunications firms and basic materials
firms with other basic materials firms. Departures from this expectation are analytically important
because they highlight companies whose attributes place them closer to a different peer group.
Agreement between the selected cluster assignments and the official JSE sector labels was
limited but non-zero, with an Adjusted Rand Index of 0.13 and Normalised Mutual Information of
0.41. These values indicate that the discovered clusters share some structure with the official sector
classification without reproducing it. This supports the interpretation that the model recovers
expected economic groupings while also surfacing cross-sector similarities and sector-divergent
anomalies.

The SHAP surrogate analysis supports the usefulness of combining clustering with interpretability.
A cluster label alone provides limited insight. Feature-level explanations show whether a group
is being formed by profitability, valuation, market size, liquidity, return behaviour or volatility.
Cluster 3, for example, is characterised by large market capitalisation, shares outstanding, trading
volume and revenue, pointing to economic scale and liquidity. Cluster 6 is driven by dividend yield,
revenue, beta and volatility while showing weak recent return, making it more outlier-like. Cluster 5

Hotelling’s T 2 analysis, a standard multivariate test for detecting observations far from the
sample centroid [25], provides a second outlier view. Scores were calculated in the two-dimensional
reduced representation of the equity dataset. Since the analysis was performed after dimensionality
reduction, the threshold was computed with n = 40 equities and p = 2 dimensions. At the 5%
significance level, the resulting critical value was approximately T 2 = 6 .66. Firms above this
threshold were flagged as multivariate outliers. Outlier status is not a buy or sell signal; it is a
prompt for further investigation.

A substantial proportion of outliers are basic materials firms, which is reasonable given
commodity-linked volatility and global pricing cycles. However, the list also includes Capitec,
Growthpoint Properties, RMH and Anheuser Busch Inbev, showing that outlier behaviour is not
restricted to mining and resources. The overlap between cluster and outlier evidence is analytically
useful: Glencore, Sasol and RMH are notable because their cluster positions and T 2 scores both
suggest unusual behaviour.
RMH warrants brief discussion because, following the 2020 unbundling of its FirstRand
shareholding, it transitioned from a large financial holding company to a substantially smaller
investment holding company with a primary focus on property investments [ 26, 27]. Its placement
in a two-company cluster together with Sasol, combined with the second-highest Hotelling’s T 2
score, is consistent with financial characteristics that differ materially from those of the broader
large-cap equity universe. Rather than indicating an error in the clustering process, this result
illustrates the intended role of the proposed screening framework: to identify companies whose
multivariate financial profiles warrant further investigation.
Because theT 2 scores were computed in the two-dimensional reduced space, they inherit the
representational limitations of that space. The flagged companies should therefore be interpreted
as outliers within the learned representation rather than as definitive outliers in the original
11-dimensional feature space. The agreement between some T 2 results and the cluster structure,
for example Glencore appearing as both a singleton cluster and the highest-scoring T 2 observation,
provides supporting evidence within the present analysis.

4.4 Robustness and practical reading
The selected t-SNE hierarchical solution was stable across the tested random seeds, with ARI
and NMI equal to 1.0. This does not remove all uncertainty, but it reduces one concern about
stochastic embeddings: that the grouping is merely a random artefact of initialisation. The results
should nevertheless be read as exploratory. A different time window, macroeconomic feature set
or weighting of variables could change the cluster structure. The value of the method lies in
repeatable screening, where changes in group membership or outlier status can be monitored over
time.
5 DISCUSSION
The results support the view that unsupervised learning can reveal meaningful structure in the
JSE Top 40. The strongest pipeline was t-SNE combined with hierarchical clustering, which is
plausible because financial variables often have non-linear relationships and t-SNE is designed to
preserve local neighbourhood structure. Hierarchical clustering then provides a representation of
nested similarity between firms.
The model does not simply reproduce official sector classifications. This is a strength rather
than a weakness. If clusters matched sectors perfectly, the method would add little beyond existing
labels. The value lies in identifying where companies behave like sector peers and where they do
not. Cluster 5 confirms sector structure in basic materials. Cluster 1 shows how some consumer
services firms can group with financial institutions, while Cluster 4 shows a financial firm and
consumer-goods firms appearing with telecommunications names. Cluster 6 and Cluster 7 show
stronger anomaly behaviour, with Sasol and RMH forming a small mixed cluster and Glencore
standing alone.
For investment management, the results should be used as a screening layer rather than a final
decision rule. A sector-divergent anomaly does not prove that a stock is mispriced and does not
imply a buy or sell signal. It identifies a company whose financial profile does not behave like
its official sector peer group. This is useful because anomaly detection helps reveal what is not
obvious from sector labels alone and gives equity analysts a structured reason to dig deeper. If
British American Tobacco is grouped with a telecommunications-related cluster, the next step is
not to reclassify the company, but to investigate which attributes such as scale, liquidity, valuation,
volatility, recent return or analyst rating created the similarity during the selected period.
The article also demonstrates why interpretability is necessary in unsupervised financial analysis.
Internal metrics indicate whether clusters are compact and separated, but not what they mean.
SHAP explanations translate cluster assignments into interpretable feature-level themes, while
Hotelling’s T 2 identifies firms far from the multivariate centre irrespective of cluster membership.
Together these components make the output easier to interrogate in an analyst workflow.
The South African contribution is important because emerging markets can differ from larger
developed markets in sector concentration, liquidity, index composition, global exposure and
macroeconomic sensitivity. A method tested only on large international markets may not transfer

Three methodological lessons emerge. First, representation matters: the best results came
from a non-linear embedding combined with hierarchical clustering rather than a linear PCA
projection. Second, validation must be layered: internal metrics, stability testing, sector ground-
truth comparison, SHAP interpretation and outlier analysis answer different questions. Third,
interpretability should be designed into the workflow from the start. Without explanation, cluster
labels may be difficult for practitioners to trust or use.
6 LIMITATIONS AND FUTURE WORK
The study has several limitations. First, the sample is limited to the JSE Top 40, so findings should
not be generalised to the entire South African equity market without further testing. Mid-cap and
small-cap companies may display different patterns because they are less liquid and may have
different risk profiles. In addition, one company in the source-provided universe, RMH, was not
an official index constituent during the window and its results should be read as those of a smaller
listed firm within a large-cap peer group rather than an index member. Second, the analysis uses
a fixed one-year snapshot from 6 February 2023 to 5 February 2024. It does not test temporal
stability across multiple regimes and one trading year may not capture downturns, bull markets
or commodity cycles. Third, t-SNE is stochastic and sensitive to parameter choices, even though
the selected solution was stable under the tested random seeds. Future work should also compare
cluster-quality and outlier results calculated in the reduced representations with corresponding
measures calculated in the original scaled feature space.
Fourth, sector labels are useful as an external ground-truth benchmark but are imperfect.
Some firms have diversified operations, global exposure or hybrid business models that do not fit
neatly into one sector. A sector mismatch should therefore be treated as a signal for investigation
rather than proof that the model is wrong. Fifth, the study does not conduct portfolio backtesting
and does not claim that identified anomalies produce excess returns. Sixth, the surrogate SHAP
approach explains a cluster-assignment model rather than the clustering algorithm itself; future
work should report surrogate fidelity and compare alternative explanation methods. Cluster-level
explanations covered seven of the eight clusters, while the singleton Glencore cluster was interpreted
through its cluster structure and Hotelling’s T 2 score. Seventh, macroeconomic variables, sentiment
data, factor exposures, ESG data or earnings revisions could change the cluster structure.
Future work should extend the dataset to more equities and longer periods, test rolling-window
clustering stability, incorporate macroeconomic and sentiment variables, and evaluate whether
cluster changes have predictive value for risk or return. For portfolio management, the most
important extension would be to test whether cluster-based diversification improves risk-adjusted
outcomes compared with sector-based diversification.

7 CONCLUSION
This article presented an interpretable unsupervised learning pipeline for detecting patterns and
anomalies among JSE Top 40 equities. The best-performing model combined t-SNE dimensionality
reduction with hierarchical clustering, achieving a Silhouette Score of 0.5023 and a Davies-Bouldin
Index of 0.5296. JSE sector labels were used as an external ground-truth benchmark and the
model identified both expected sector-aligned groupings and non-obvious mixed-sector clusters.
The main finding is that unsupervised learning can complement traditional sector-based
analysis by identifying hidden similarities, sector-divergent anomalies and multivariate outliers.
SHAP-based interpretation and Hotelling’s T 2 analysis make the results more useful by explaining
influential variables and highlighting firms that deserve further investigation. The practical value is
not that the model replaces equity analysts, but that it helps them notice unexpected relationships
and then investigate the financial attributes behind those results. The study contributes a South
African emerging-market case study and demonstrates a practical framework for interpretable
financial clustering.
